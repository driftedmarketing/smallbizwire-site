The cold-email deliverability checklist

Twenty checks to run before your next outbound campaign, from DNS records to the wording of your first line.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Before any cold-email campaign, confirm one SPF record, passing DKIM, a published DMARC record and alignment; use a warmed, separate sending domain; verify every address; keep volume steady per inbox; write short, specific plain-text messages with an easy opt-out; and watch bounces, complaints and replies daily, pausing at the first warning sign.

Use this as a pre-flight list. Every item is something that commonly causes good emails to land in spam or bounce.

Domain and DNS

  1. One valid SPF record covering every service that sends for the domain.
  2. DKIM enabled for each sending service, and passing.
  3. A DMARC record published, at minimum p=none with a reporting address.
  4. The "From" domain aligns with the domain that signs DKIM.
  5. The outreach domain has a working website or redirect, so it does not look abandoned.
  6. Custom tracking domain configured, if your tool tracks clicks, rather than a shared one.

Mailboxes

  1. Each mailbox has been warmed for several weeks (see warm-up guide).
  2. A real name, photo and signature on each sender.
  3. Daily volume per mailbox held steady, not spiking.
  4. Replies are monitored by a person every working day.

List

  1. Every address verified with a verification service before sending.
  2. No purchased or scraped "bulk" lists.
  3. Role addresses (info@, sales@) used sparingly; they bounce and complain more.
  4. A suppression list of past unsubscribes and bounces applied to every campaign.

Message

  1. Plain text, short, with at most one link and no attachments on first touch.
  2. The first line is about the recipient, and true.
  3. No spam-trigger theatrics: all-caps subject lines, excess punctuation, fake "Re:" prefixes.
  4. A clear, easy way to opt out, and it is honoured promptly.
  5. Your business name and postal address included where the law requires them.

Measurement

  1. Track bounces, reply rate and positive reply rate per campaign; pause anything with rising bounces or complaints, and check Google Postmaster Tools.

The point of all this

Deliverability is not a trick. Mailbox providers are trying to deliver mail people want. Every item above is either proving who you are or showing that recipients welcome your messages. If you are short on time, prioritise authentication, list verification and relevance, in that order.

How do I use this checklist?

Run through it before the first send of every campaign, not once a year. Most deliverability problems are introduced by a change: a new sending tool, a bought list, a new template with heavy tracking. Tick each item off or write down why it does not apply.

What should I check on the day of sending?

  1. Send a test to your own Gmail and Outlook addresses and confirm they land in the inbox.
  2. Click every link and confirm none point to a broken or unrelated page.
  3. Check personalisation fields for blanks; "Hi {first_name}" undoes everything else.
  4. Confirm the sending schedule spreads messages through the working day rather than in one burst.
  5. Confirm that replies stop the sequence for that contact.

What should I watch in the first 72 hours?

  • Bounce rate. If it is clearly higher than your test lists, stop and re-verify.
  • Spam complaints. Any visible rise is a reason to pause.
  • Reply rate. A sudden drop compared with earlier campaigns can mean spam-folder placement.
  • Out-of-office and auto-replies. These confirm delivery and sometimes reveal a better contact.

What are the most common mistakes?

  • Sending from the main business domain, so a bad campaign hurts invoices and customer mail.
  • Using bought or scraped lists without verification.
  • Heavy HTML, images and multiple tracking links in a first-touch email.
  • Long sequences that keep emailing people who never engaged.
  • Scaling volume the week warm-up ends.

What should I record after each campaign?

Keep a simple log: date, domain, inboxes used, number sent, bounces, complaints, replies and anything you changed. Over a few campaigns this log tells you which domains are healthy, which templates work and which list sources to drop. It is also the first thing to read when results suddenly fall.

Finally, remember that the message itself is part of deliverability. A short note that clearly relates to the recipient's business earns replies, and replies are among the best signals a sender can earn. Write for one person, not for a list.

How do I keep my list clean between campaigns?

Lists decay. People change jobs, companies close and inboxes are abandoned, so an address that was valid six months ago may bounce today. Re-verify any list older than a few months before reusing it. Remove hard bounces immediately and never retry them. Add anyone who asks to stop to a suppression list that every tool and every domain checks before sending, so a person who opted out on one domain is not contacted from another. Remove contacts who have received a full sequence without opening or replying; continuing to email them adds risk and almost no chance of a reply. Finally, record where each list came from. When one source produces high bounces or complaints, you can drop it without discarding the rest. A smaller list of the right people will usually outperform a bigger list of uncertain ones, both in replies and in the long-term health of your domains.

Questions readers ask

What is a good bounce rate for cold email?

As low as you can get it. Verify every address first; a noticeably higher bounce rate than your previous campaigns is a reason to stop and clean the list.

Should cold emails include images or tracking?

Keep first-touch emails plain text with few or no links. Heavy HTML, images and tracking links look like bulk marketing and can hurt placement.

How often should I run this checklist?

Before every campaign, and whenever you change a sending tool, domain, list source or template.

Is cold email legal?

Rules vary by country. In the US, CAN-SPAM allows commercial email with honest headers, a physical address and a working opt-out; stricter consent rules apply in places such as the EU and Canada.
