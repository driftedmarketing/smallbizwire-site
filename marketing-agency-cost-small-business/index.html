How much does a marketing agency cost for a small business?

Retainers, project fees and percent-of-spend, explained, with rough ranges and the costs that sit outside the quote.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Most small businesses pay an agency in one of three ways: a monthly retainer, a fixed project fee, or a percentage of ad spend. Rough, highly variable ranges run from a few hundred dollars a month for a narrow freelance-style service to several thousand for a multi-channel agency. Ad spend itself is almost always billed separately.

There is no list price for marketing. Two agencies quoting the same small business can be several times apart, and both can be fair, because they are pricing different amounts of work, different seniority and different risk. What you can control is understanding how the price is built, so that you compare like with like and know what you are actually buying.

A word on the numbers below: they are rough, illustrative ranges drawn from how agencies commonly price, not survey results. They vary widely by country, city, channel and the agency's own positioning. Treat them as a way to sanity-check a quote, not as a benchmark to negotiate against.

What are the main ways agencies charge?

Nearly every quote you receive will be one of three models, or a blend of them.

Monthly retainer

A retainer is a fixed fee each month for an agreed scope of ongoing work: managing ad campaigns, producing a set number of social posts, running email, reporting. It is the most common model for continuing marketing because both sides can plan around it. The risk for the client is paying the same fee in a month when less was done; the protection is a written scope that says what the fee buys.

Project fee

A project fee is a fixed price for a defined deliverable with an end: a website, a brand identity, a store setup, a launch campaign, a set of videos. It suits work that can be specified up front. The risk is scope creep, where the project grows and either the agency absorbs the cost or you face change-order charges, so the scope and the number of revision rounds need to be in writing.

Percentage of ad spend

Some paid-media agencies charge a percentage of the advertising budget they manage, often with a monthly minimum. It scales the fee with the size of the account, which makes sense because a larger account takes more work. The weakness is an incentive to recommend higher spend. Ask whether the percentage applies to all spend or tiers down as budgets grow, and insist that the ad account stays in your name.

Hourly and performance-based

Hourly billing is more common for freelancers and consultants than agencies. Performance-based pricing, where fees depend on results such as leads or sales, exists but is rarer than sales pages suggest, because results depend on things the agency does not control, such as your pricing, stock and how fast you answer leads. If you are offered it, read exactly how a result is counted.

What is usually not included in the fee?

The agency's fee is rarely the whole cost. Budget separately for:

  • Ad spend. The money paid to Meta, Google, TikTok or others. This should be charged by the platform to your own card or account, not marked up and resold.
  • Software. Email platforms, scheduling tools, SEO tools, Shopify apps and stock libraries often sit on your bill.
  • Production. Photography, filming days, models, locations, props and travel.
  • Setup or onboarding fees. A one-off charge for audits, account setup and strategy at the start.
  • Extra revisions or rush work. Often billed beyond the agreed rounds.

How do you tell whether a quote is fair?

A fair quote is one where you can see what you are paying for. Ask the agency to break the fee into the people involved, the hours or deliverables per month and the tools included. Compare that breakdown across two or three agencies, not the headline number. A cheap quote that hides a junior team or a lock-in clause can cost more than an expensive one.

Also weigh the cost against what the work must return. If a retainer costs more each month than the extra gross profit you could reasonably expect it to generate within a few months, the maths does not work at your current size, however good the agency is. In that case a smaller scope, a single channel or a freelancer may be the right step, as we discuss in freelancer vs agency vs in-house.

How should a small business budget for its first agency?

  1. Start with one channel. Pick the one closest to revenue, often paid search or paid social for e-commerce, or local SEO for service businesses.
  2. Set a test period. Three months is a common minimum for paid media to learn and be judged; SEO usually needs longer.
  3. Agree one measure of success and a date, in writing, before the work starts.
  4. Keep the ad budget separate and in your own accounts so you can stop or switch agencies without losing history.
  5. Review monthly against the agreed measure, not against how busy the reports look.

Is cheaper ever the better choice?

Sometimes. A small business with a modest budget is often better served by a focused specialist who does one thing well than by a broad agency spreading a small fee across many channels. The question is not "what is the lowest price" but "what is the smallest piece of work that could move revenue, and who does that well." Questions readers ask

What is a typical marketing agency retainer for a small business?

Roughly several hundred to a few thousand dollars a month, depending on channels, volume, seniority and location. These ranges vary widely and are not survey figures.

Is ad spend included in an agency's fee?

Usually not. Ad spend is paid to the platform separately, ideally from an account in your own name.

Is percent-of-spend pricing a good deal?

It can be, but it rewards higher spend. Ask whether the rate tiers down and whether there is a monthly minimum.

How long should I give an agency before judging results?

For paid media, about three months is a common minimum; SEO typically takes longer. Agree the measure and date in writing first.

What hidden costs should I expect?

Software, production such as shoots, setup fees, and extra revisions are commonly billed outside the main fee.
