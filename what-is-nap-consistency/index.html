What is NAP consistency and why does it matter for local SEO?

NAP stands for name, address and phone number. When they differ across the web, customers and search engines both get confused.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: NAP consistency means your business name, address and phone number appear the same way everywhere they are listed online: your website, Google Business Profile, directories and social profiles. Consistent details help search engines confidently match listings to one real business, and they stop customers calling an old number or driving to an old address.

NAP is one of the oldest ideas in local search, and it remains one of the most practical. The acronym stands for name, address and phone number. Some people add a W for website, making it NAPW. The idea is that these core details should be identical, or as close to identical as possible, everywhere your business appears online.

This article explains why that matters, what counts as inconsistent, and how to audit and fix your listings without spending a fortune.

Why does NAP consistency matter?

There are two audiences: people and search engines.

For people, the reason is obvious. If a directory lists your old phone number, someone who finds you there calls a dead line or, worse, your previous tenant. If an old address appears on a map app, a customer drives to the wrong place and is unlikely to give you a second chance.

For search engines, the reason is about confidence. Google and other search engines gather information about local businesses from many sources: your Business Profile, your website, directories, data aggregators and more. When those sources agree, it is easier to be confident they describe one real business at one real location. When they disagree, the picture gets muddier. Google lists prominence, which it describes as how well known a business is, including information from across the web, among the factors in local ranking.

How much NAP consistency directly moves rankings today is debated among practitioners, and nobody outside Google can measure it precisely. What is not in doubt is that wrong details cost real customers. That alone justifies the work.

What counts as an inconsistency?

Some differences matter more than others:

Type of differenceExampleHow serious
Wrong or old phone numberA number you stopped using last yearSerious; loses calls
Wrong or old addressYour previous premisesSerious; loses visits and confuses maps
Different business nameAn old trading name or added keywordsSerious; can look like two businesses
Formatting differences"Street" vs "St.", "Suite 4" vs "#4"Minor; search engines usually handle these

Do not lose sleep over abbreviations. Focus first on anything that is factually wrong.

What should my "official" NAP be?

Before you fix anything, decide on a single, canonical version and write it down:

  • Name: your real-world business name, as on your signage and paperwork. Do not add keywords or locations that are not part of the name; Google's guidelines for Business Profiles specifically warn against this.
  • Address: formatted the way your national postal service formats it. If you are a service-area business that does not serve customers at your address, you may hide the address on Google; see our local SEO checklist for service-area businesses.
  • Phone: a local number where possible, the one you actually answer.
  • Website: the exact main URL, consistently with or without "www."

Every listing you create or correct from now on should match this record.

Where does my NAP appear online?

More places than most owners realise. Common ones include:

  • Your Google Business Profile.
  • Your own website, especially the footer and contact page.
  • Apple Business Connect, which feeds Apple Maps, and Bing Places for Business.
  • Social profiles such as Facebook and Instagram.
  • General directories and review sites relevant to your country.
  • Industry-specific directories and trade associations.
  • Local chamber of commerce, council or community listings.
  • Old listings created automatically by data providers that you never claimed.

How do I audit my listings?

  1. Search for your business name in Google, then your name plus your city, and note every listing on the first few pages.
  2. Search for your old phone numbers and old addresses. This finds listings that use your previous details, which a name search can miss.
  3. Check the major maps platforms directly: Google, Apple and Bing.
  4. Record everything in a spreadsheet: the site, the URL, what it shows, whether you control it, and what needs fixing.
  5. Prioritise by how many people are likely to see each listing. The big maps platforms first, then the directories that rank for your name.

Paid listing-management services can scan and update many directories at once. They can save time, especially for multi-location businesses, but they are not required for a small business with a handful of listings. If you use one, check what happens to your listings if you stop paying.

How do I fix incorrect listings?

  • Listings you control: log in and update them.
  • Listings you can claim: most directories have a "claim this business" process, often with verification by phone, email or post.
  • Listings you cannot claim: look for a "suggest an edit" or contact option.
  • Duplicates: if Google shows two profiles for your business, Google's support documentation explains how to request that duplicates be merged or removed.

Corrections can take weeks to show up, and some directories pull data from aggregators, so an old number may reappear until the source is fixed. Keep the spreadsheet and recheck after a month or two.

What about call tracking numbers?

Many businesses want to know which listing or campaign generated a call, so they use different tracking numbers. Using many different numbers across directories works against NAP consistency. A common approach is to keep your main number as the primary number on your Google Business Profile and directories, and use tracking numbers only in ads or campaigns where you control them. Google Business Profile also allows additional phone numbers; check its current guidance before setting this up.

What should I do when I move or change my number?

This is when consistency breaks most often. Make a list from your spreadsheet before the change, update the maps platforms and your website on the day, and work through directories within the following weeks. Keep the old number forwarding for a while if you can.

NAP consistency is not exciting, but it is foundational. It sits underneath everything in our guide to Google Business Profile and local SEO, and it supports the prominence factor covered in our article on ranking in the Google map pack.

Questions readers ask

What does NAP stand for?

Name, address and phone number. Some people add website and call it NAPW.

Do abbreviations like St. versus Street matter?

Usually not much. Search engines generally handle formatting differences. Focus on details that are actually wrong, such as an old number or address.

Should I use a tracking number on my Google Business Profile?

Be careful. A common approach is to keep your main number as primary and use tracking numbers only in campaigns you control. Check Google's current guidance.

Do I need a paid listing management service?

Not usually for a small business with a few listings. It can save time for multi-location businesses. Check what happens to listings if you stop paying.

How long do corrections take to appear?

Often weeks. Some directories pull data from aggregators, so recheck after a month or two and fix the source if old details return.
