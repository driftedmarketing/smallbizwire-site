SPF, DKIM and DMARC, explained for people who just want their email delivered

Three DNS records decide whether Gmail and Outlook trust mail from your domain. Here is what each one does and how to set them up.

SmallBizWire Editors · 30 October 2026 · 2 min read

If your invoices land in spam or your cold emails vanish, the cause is often not your wording. It is that the receiving mail server cannot verify the message really came from you. Three records in your domain's DNS settings solve that: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

SPF: who is allowed to send

A Sender Policy Framework record is a single line of text (a TXT record) listing the services permitted to send mail for your domain, such as Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or your newsletter tool. A typical record looks like v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all.

  • You may have only one SPF record per domain. If you add a new sending tool, edit the existing record rather than creating a second one.
  • SPF allows at most ten DNS lookups. Stacking many include: entries can silently break it.

DKIM: a signature on every message

DomainKeys Identified Mail adds a cryptographic signature to each email. Your mail provider generates a key pair; you publish the public half in DNS (usually as a TXT or CNAME record under a name like google._domainkey), and the provider signs outgoing mail with the private half. Receivers check that the signature matches, which proves the message was not altered and was authorised by your domain.

Each sending service needs its own DKIM setup. Turning it on in Google Workspace does not cover your email marketing platform.

DMARC: the policy that ties them together

DMARC tells receivers what to do when a message fails SPF and DKIM alignment, and where to send reports. Start gently:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com

With p=none, nothing is blocked; you simply receive reports showing who is sending as your domain. Once the reports show only your legitimate services, move to p=quarantine and eventually p=reject, which also protects you from people spoofing your domain.

Why this is no longer optional

In 2024 Google and Yahoo began enforcing requirements for bulk senders (Google defines these as those sending around 5,000 or more messages a day to personal Gmail accounts): authenticate with SPF and DKIM, publish a DMARC record, offer one-click unsubscribe on marketing mail, and keep reported spam rates low. Smaller senders are expected to authenticate too. Without these records, even modest volumes get filtered.

How to check your setup

  1. Send a message to a Gmail address, open it, and choose "Show original." Look for SPF, DKIM and DMARC each marked PASS.
  2. Use a free checker such as MXToolbox or Google's Admin Toolbox to inspect the records.
  3. Read your first DMARC reports after a week. Unknown senders are either a tool you forgot or someone impersonating you.

Questions readers ask

Do I need SPF, DKIM and DMARC for a small business?

Yes. Major mailbox providers expect all senders to authenticate, and bulk senders to Gmail and Yahoo are required to.

Can I have two SPF records?

No. A domain should have exactly one SPF record. Combine all sending services into it.

What DMARC policy should I start with?

Start with p=none to collect reports, then move to quarantine and reject once only legitimate senders appear.
