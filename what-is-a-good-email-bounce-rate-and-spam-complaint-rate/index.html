What is a good email bounce rate and spam complaint rate?

Google's published 0.3% spam rate ceiling, commonly cited bounce benchmarks, and what to do when your numbers slip.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Google asks bulk senders to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%, and to aim well under it, commonly around 0.1%. For bounces there is no single official limit, but many senders treat under about 2% as healthy. Rising numbers usually point to old lists, missing permission or poor list hygiene.

Two numbers tell you more about the health of your email than open rates ever will: how many messages bounce, and how many recipients mark you as spam. Mailbox providers watch both closely, and when they rise, your mail starts landing in spam folders. This guide explains what each number means, which thresholds are published, which are just industry rules of thumb, and how to bring them down.

What is a bounce?

A bounce is an email that could not be delivered, returned with an error. There are two kinds:

  • Hard bounces are permanent: the address does not exist, the domain is gone, or the recipient's server has refused you permanently. Remove these addresses immediately.
  • Soft bounces are temporary: a full mailbox, a server outage, or a message too large. Most sending tools retry, and stop after repeated failures.

Bounce rate is usually calculated as bounced messages divided by messages sent, for a campaign or period.

What is a spam complaint rate?

A spam complaint happens when a recipient clicks "Report spam" or "Junk." The complaint rate is complaints divided by messages delivered. Many providers share complaint data with senders through feedback programmes, and Google shows a spam rate for your domain in Google Postmaster Tools.

What does Google say about spam rates?

Google's published sender guidelines, tightened for bulk senders from 2024, ask senders to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%. Google also recommends staying lower, and the figure often cited from its guidance as a target is under 0.1%. Senders who stay above the ceiling can expect more of their mail to be filtered or rejected. Yahoo announced similar expectations for bulk senders at the same time.

Put simply, 0.3% means three complaints for every thousand delivered emails. That is a small number, which is why sending to people who did not expect your mail is so damaging.

What is a good bounce rate?

There is no single official bounce threshold published by the major mailbox providers in the way Google publishes its spam rate. Email platforms and deliverability practitioners commonly suggest the following as rough guides. Treat them as rules of thumb, not standards, and check your own sending platform's guidance, since many platforms set their own limits and may pause accounts that exceed them.

Why do these numbers matter so much?

Mailbox providers use them as evidence of whether you have permission to email people. A sender with a clean list of people who signed up rarely sees many bounces or complaints. A sender working from an old, bought or scraped list sees both. Providers respond by filtering more of your mail, which lowers engagement, which worsens reputation further. Our guide to why emails go to spam walks through that cycle.

What causes a high bounce rate?

  • Old lists. People change jobs and abandon addresses. A list that has not been mailed for a year will bounce noticeably.
  • Typos at sign-up. "gmial.com" and similar mistakes.
  • Purchased or scraped lists. Often full of invalid and guessed addresses.
  • Guessed addresses in cold outreach. Patterns like firstname@company that do not exist.
  • Authentication failures that cause receiving servers to reject mail, which can show up as bounces.

What causes a high complaint rate?

  • Recipients who do not remember signing up, or never did.
  • Sending far more often than people expected.
  • Content that does not match what they signed up for.
  • An unsubscribe link that is hidden or broken, so the spam button is easier.
  • Unsolicited cold email sent at volume.

How do you lower your bounce rate?

  1. Remove hard bounces immediately, and let your tool suppress repeat soft bounces.
  2. Use confirmed opt-in, where new subscribers click a link to confirm, to catch typos and fake sign-ups.
  3. Verify older lists with an email verification service before mailing them.
  4. Mail your list regularly so addresses do not go stale unnoticed.
  5. Check that SPF, DKIM and DMARC are passing.

How do you lower your complaint rate?

  1. Only email people who asked to hear from you.
  2. Remind people why they are receiving the email, near the top.
  3. Make unsubscribing obvious, and support one-click unsubscribe, which Google and Yahoo expect from bulk senders.
  4. Honour unsubscribes promptly.
  5. Send at a consistent, expected frequency.
  6. Stop mailing people who have not engaged for a long time, after a re-engagement attempt.

Where do you find your numbers?

Your newsletter or sending platform reports bounces and, usually, complaints per campaign. Google Postmaster Tools shows your domain's spam rate with Gmail users once you send enough volume to Gmail. Microsoft offers its own sender data programmes for Outlook recipients. Check these regularly, not only when something goes wrong. A monthly look at bounces and complaints side by side will usually show a problem forming weeks before it turns into mail landing in spam folders.

What about cold email?

Cold outreach is especially sensitive, because recipients did not sign up. A handful of complaints on a small send can push you over 0.3%. Keep sends small and targeted, verify addresses, and read the cold email deliverability checklist before scaling.

The bottom line

Keep complaints under Google's published 0.3% ceiling and ideally near 0.1%, and keep bounces low by mailing clean, permission-based lists. Both numbers are signs of permission. Earn it, and both stay low.

Questions readers ask

What spam complaint rate does Google allow?

Google's bulk sender guidelines ask senders to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%, and recommend staying lower, often cited as under 0.1%.

Is there an official bounce rate limit?

Not a single published one from the major mailbox providers. Many senders treat total bounces under about 2% as healthy, and sending platforms may set their own limits.

What is the difference between a hard and soft bounce?

A hard bounce is permanent, such as an address that does not exist. A soft bounce is temporary, such as a full mailbox.

How do I see my Gmail spam rate?

Set up Google Postmaster Tools for your domain. It shows your spam rate once you send enough mail to Gmail users.

Will one bad campaign ruin my reputation?

It can hurt, but steady, permission-based sending afterwards usually helps recover over time. Remove bounces and find the cause of the complaints quickly.
