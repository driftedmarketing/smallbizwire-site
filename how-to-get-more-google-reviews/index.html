How do I get more Google reviews without breaking the rules?

Asking every customer is allowed. Paying for reviews, offering discounts for them, or only asking happy customers is not.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Ask every customer, at the right moment, with a direct link to your review form, and make it quick. Google's policies prohibit offering incentives for reviews and selectively asking only satisfied customers, a practice called review gating. Reply to every review, good and bad, and never post fake reviews or have staff review the business.

Reviews on your Google Business Profile do two jobs at once. They help potential customers decide whether to trust you, and Google lists review count and score among the things that contribute to a business's prominence in local results. So it is natural to want more of them.

The problem is that many common tactics break Google's rules, and the consequences can include reviews being removed, restrictions on your profile, or in some cases warnings shown to the public. In several countries, fake or misleading reviews are also a consumer protection issue. This guide covers what is allowed, what is not, and a simple system for steadily earning genuine reviews.

Google's policies change over time, so read the current version of its prohibited and restricted content policy for maps reviews before you set up any process. What follows reflects its long-standing positions.

What does Google prohibit when it comes to reviews?

  • Incentives. Offering money, discounts, free products, entry into a prize draw or anything else in exchange for a review. This applies even if you do not ask for a positive review.
  • Review gating. Asking customers first whether they had a good experience, then only sending the happy ones to Google and the unhappy ones to a private feedback form.
  • Fake reviews. Reviews from people who were not customers, reviews you write yourself, or reviews bought from a service.
  • Conflicts of interest. Owners or staff reviewing their own business, or reviewing competitors.
  • Bulk or on-site review stations that produce many reviews from one device or location can be flagged as suspicious, so let customers review on their own devices.

Some review platforms other than Google have their own, sometimes stricter, rules about asking at all. Check each platform's policy separately.

What is allowed?

Quite a lot. You can:

  • Ask every customer for a review, as long as you ask them all, not only the ones you expect to be happy.
  • Make it easy with a direct link or QR code to your review form.
  • Remind customers once, politely, if they have not left one.
  • Reply publicly to reviews.
  • Flag reviews that genuinely break Google's policies, such as spam or off-topic content, for Google to assess.

The principle is simple: you may invite feedback, but you may not buy it, filter it or fake it.

How do I get my review link?

Google lets you share a short link that opens the review form for your business directly. You can find it from your Business Profile, usually under an option labelled something like "Ask for reviews" or "Get more reviews." The exact menu wording has changed over the years, so look in your profile's management view in Google Search or Maps. Google can also generate a QR code for the same link.

Use that one link everywhere. Do not send customers to your profile and hope they find the review button.

When is the best time to ask for a review?

Ask when the customer has just experienced the value and the memory is fresh:

Restaurant or caféAs the bill is settledA small card or QR code on the table or receipt
Tradesperson or home serviceWhen the job is finished and checkedA text message with the link the same day
Clinic, salon or studioAfter the appointmentA follow-up email or text
Online shop with local pickupA few days after delivery or collectionAn order follow-up email

What should the request say?

Short, personal and specific. A good request:

  1. Thanks them by name.
  2. Mentions what you did for them.
  3. Asks for an honest review, not a five-star one.
  4. Includes the direct link.
  5. Says it takes a minute or two.

For example: "Thanks again for choosing us for your kitchen repair, Sam. If you have a minute, we'd really value an honest review on Google. It helps other people nearby find us. Here's the link." Asking for an "honest" review keeps you clearly on the right side of the rules and tends to read as more trustworthy.

How do I build this into a routine?

Reviews come from consistency, not from occasional campaigns. A burst of reviews followed by silence can also look odd. Set a simple routine:

  • Decide the single moment you will ask, for every customer.
  • Add the request to your checklist, booking system, or invoicing tool so it happens automatically or as part of a habit.
  • Train staff to mention it verbally too, without pressure.
  • Send one reminder after a few days to anyone who has not reviewed. Then stop.
  • Check monthly how many requests went out and how many reviews came in.

How should I reply to reviews?

Reply to every review, positive and negative. Replies show prospective customers that a real person is paying attention.

  • Positive reviews: thank them, mention something specific from their review, and keep it short.
  • Negative reviews: stay calm and polite, acknowledge the experience, avoid sharing private details, and offer to resolve it offline with a contact method. Future customers read how you handle complaints more closely than the complaint itself.
  • Reviews that break policy: you can flag them for Google to assess. Google decides; it does not remove reviews simply because they are negative.

Never offer a customer something in exchange for changing or removing a review. That is also an incentive.

What about review software and agencies?

Many tools automate review requests, and plenty work within the rules. Before using one, check that it sends requests to all customers rather than filtering by a satisfaction score first. Avoid any service that offers to supply reviews. If something sounds like a shortcut, it probably breaks the policy.

Questions readers ask

Can I offer a discount for a Google review?

No. Google's policies prohibit offering incentives of any kind in exchange for reviews, even if you do not ask for a positive one.

What is review gating?

Asking customers whether they were happy first, then only sending satisfied ones to Google. Google prohibits it. Ask every customer instead.

Can my staff leave reviews for the business?

No. Reviews from owners or employees are a conflict of interest under Google's policies.

Can I get a negative review removed?

Only if it breaks Google's policies, such as spam or off-topic content. You can flag it, and Google decides. Replying calmly is usually the better response.

Is it okay to send a reminder?

Yes. One polite reminder to customers who have not left a review is fine. Repeated pressure is not a good look.
