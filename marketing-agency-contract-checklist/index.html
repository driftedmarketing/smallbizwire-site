What should be in a marketing agency contract?

Scope, fees, ownership, access, termination and the clauses small businesses most often forget.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: A marketing agency contract should state the scope of work, fees and payment terms, what counts as success and how it is reported, who owns the ad accounts, content and data, notice and termination terms, confidentiality, revision limits and how disputes are handled. Anything said on a sales call but missing from the contract is not promised.

Most disputes between small businesses and agencies are not about bad faith. They are about two people remembering a sales call differently. A good contract turns that call into a written record both sides can point to. You do not need a long document, but you do need the right clauses. This guide is general information, not legal advice; for anything significant, have a lawyer in your jurisdiction review the agreement.

What is the difference between a contract and a statement of work?

Many agencies use two documents. A master services agreement (MSA) sets the legal terms that apply to the whole relationship: liability, confidentiality, ownership, termination. A statement of work (SOW) describes a specific piece of work: deliverables, timeline, fees. This lets you add new projects with a new SOW without renegotiating everything. Either structure is fine, as long as everything below is covered somewhere.

What should the scope of work say?

The scope is the most important part and the most often vague. It should list:

  • Deliverables with numbers. "Twelve social posts and four short videos a month," not "social media management."
  • Channels covered and those explicitly excluded.
  • Who provides what. Product photos, copy approvals, access, stock.
  • Revision rounds included per deliverable, and the price of extra rounds.
  • Turnaround times for approvals on both sides.
  • Named people or roles who will do the work, if that mattered in your decision.

How should fees and payment be written?

State the amount, the currency, the billing date, the payment method, and what happens if a payment is late. If ad spend is involved, say clearly that it is paid by you directly to the platform, or, if the agency pays it, how it is invoiced and whether there is any markup. List any setup fees, software costs passed through, and how price increases are handled. For agencies in another country, the currency clause matters more; see working with agencies abroad.

Who should own the accounts, content and data?

This is the clause small businesses regret skipping. The contract should say:

  • Ad accounts (Meta Business, Google Ads, TikTok) are owned by you, with the agency added as a partner or user.
  • Analytics, tag manager, domain, hosting and Shopify are in your name, and you hold the top-level login.
  • Creative and content you have paid for transfer to you, or are licensed to you without time limit, on payment. Note any exceptions, such as stock assets licensed by the agency or the agency's own templates.
  • Customer data belongs to you and must be returned or deleted at the end.
  • The agency may show the work in its portfolio only if you agree, and you can say no.

How should success and reporting be defined?

An agency cannot honestly guarantee outcomes it does not control, but it can commit to a measure, a target and a date to review against. Write down the one or two numbers that matter, how they are measured (which tool, which attribution setting), how often you get a report, and what happens if the target is missed: a review meeting, a change of plan, or a right to exit early. A clause like this keeps both sides honest without pretending results are guaranteed.

What should termination and notice say?

  • Minimum term, if any. Long lock-ins favour the agency; three months is common for paid media, with monthly rolling after.
  • Notice period to end the agreement, often 30 days.
  • Handover obligations: access removed cleanly, files and logins handed over, work in progress delivered or credited.
  • Termination for cause, such as missed payments or material breach, with a chance to fix it first.
  • What is owed on exit, including any unpaid work and prepaid fees.

Which other clauses matter?

  • Confidentiality. Both sides keep each other's business information private.
  • Compliance. The agency follows platform policies and advertising rules; you are responsible for the truth of your product claims.
  • Liability cap. Agencies usually cap their liability at the fees paid over a period. Understand the cap.
  • Non-solicitation. Some contracts stop either side hiring the other's staff for a period.
  • Governing law and disputes. Which country's or state's law applies and where disputes are heard. This matters when the agency is abroad.
  • Data protection. If the agency handles customer personal data, terms that meet the laws that apply to you.

What does a quick checklist look like?

ClauseQuestion to ask
ScopeCould a stranger tell exactly what is delivered each month?
FeesAre currency, dates, late terms and pass-through costs stated?
OwnershipDo we own every account, file and dataset?
SuccessIs there a measure, a target and a review date?
TerminationHow do we leave, and what do we get on the way out?
LawWhich jurisdiction applies if something goes wrong?

How should you negotiate the contract?

Read the draft slowly and mark every sentence you do not understand. Ask the agency to explain each one in plain words, then ask for the explanation to be written into the contract. Most agencies expect some back-and-forth, and a reasonable request, such as a shorter first term, ownership of source files or a clearer reporting clause, is rarely a deal-breaker. If a simple request is refused outright, treat that as information about how the relationship will run. Keep a copy of the signed version, every later change in writing, and a short note of what was agreed on each call, sent to the agency by email so both sides have the same record.

What should make you pause?

Be wary of a contract with no scope beyond a fee, ownership of ad accounts held by the agency, automatic renewal for long terms, penalties for leaving that exceed the remaining fees, or refusal to put verbal promises in writing. We cover more of these in red flags when hiring an agency.

Questions readers ask

Do I need a lawyer to review an agency contract?

For small, short engagements many owners do not, but for large fees, long terms or cross-border work a lawyer's review is worth it. This guide is not legal advice.

Who should own the ad account?

You should. The agency should be added as a partner or user on an account in your business's name.

What is a reasonable notice period?

Thirty days is common, sometimes after a minimum term of around three months.

Can an agency guarantee results in the contract?

Not honestly, since results depend on factors outside its control. It can commit to a measure, a target, reporting and a review date.

What is an SOW?

A statement of work: a document describing a specific project's deliverables, timeline and fees, usually under a broader services agreement.
