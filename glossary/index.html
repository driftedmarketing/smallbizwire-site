Small-business marketing glossary

35 terms from email, search, ads and AI, defined in plain English.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026

AEO

AEO, answer engine optimization, is the practice of structuring content so that search features and AI assistants can lift a clear, direct answer from it. It emphasises question-style headings, concise answers near the top, accurate facts and structured data, alongside conventional search optimization. Read the guide.

AI assistant

An AI assistant is software, usually powered by a large language model, that helps people with tasks through conversation: drafting messages, summarising documents, answering questions and organising work. For businesses, the safest pattern is that the assistant drafts and a person approves anything that is sent, paid or published. Read the guide.

AI Overviews

AI Overviews are AI-generated summaries that Google shows at the top of some search results, with links to source pages. They answer a query directly, which can reduce clicks for some searches while giving cited sources visibility. Google says the usual search best practices apply to appearing in them. Read the guide.

BIMI

Brand Indicators for Message Identification is a standard that lets a domain display its logo beside authenticated email in supporting inboxes. It requires a DMARC policy at enforcement, a logo in a specific SVG format, and for some mailbox providers a verified mark certificate. It rewards strong authentication with brand visibility.

Bounce rate (email)

In email, bounce rate is the share of sent messages that could not be delivered. Hard bounces come from invalid or non-existent addresses; soft bounces are temporary, such as a full mailbox. A high bounce rate harms sender reputation, so lists should be verified and invalid addresses removed promptly. In web analytics the term means something different. Read the guide.

CAC

CAC, customer acquisition cost, is the total cost of winning a new customer, including advertising, agency or staff costs, tools and promotions, divided by the number of new customers in the same period. Comparing CAC with lifetime value shows whether a business can afford its growth and which channels pay off.

Canonical URL

A canonical URL is the version of a page that a site tells search engines to treat as the main one when the same or very similar content is available at several addresses. It is set with a rel=canonical link tag and helps consolidate ranking signals and avoid duplicate-content confusion, common on e-commerce sites. Read the guide.

Conversion rate

Conversion rate is the percentage of visitors or recipients who complete a desired action, such as a purchase, sign-up or enquiry, out of the total who had the chance. It is calculated as conversions divided by visitors. Small improvements can have a large effect on revenue without increasing traffic or ad spend. Read the guide.

CPC

CPC, cost per click, is the average amount an advertiser pays each time someone clicks an ad. It is calculated as total spend divided by clicks. CPC varies by platform, audience, industry and competition, and a cheap click is only valuable if it leads to customers at an acceptable cost. Read the guide.

CPM

CPM, cost per mille, is the cost of one thousand ad impressions. It measures how expensive it is to reach an audience, regardless of clicks. Advertisers compare CPMs across platforms and audiences, and rising CPMs often signal more competition for the same people, for example during busy shopping seasons. Read the guide.

CTR

CTR, click-through rate, is the percentage of people who click a link, ad or search result after seeing it, calculated as clicks divided by impressions. It indicates how compelling a headline, ad or listing is to the people who see it, though a high CTR matters only if those clicks go on to convert.

DKIM

DomainKeys Identified Mail adds a cryptographic signature to outgoing email. The receiving server checks the signature against a public key published in your domain's DNS. A valid signature shows the message was authorised by the domain and was not altered in transit, which helps deliverability and protects against spoofing. Read the guide.

DMARC

Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance is a DNS policy that tells receiving servers what to do with mail that fails SPF and DKIM alignment checks: deliver it, quarantine it or reject it. It also sends reports to the domain owner, showing who is sending email using the domain. Read the guide.

GEO

GEO, generative engine optimization, refers to efforts to make content more likely to be cited or summarised by AI systems that generate answers, such as AI chat assistants and AI-powered search. The field is young and its methods are still debated; clear, well-sourced, accessible content is the common foundation. Read the guide.

Google Business Profile

Google Business Profile is Google's free listing for businesses, shown in Google Search and Maps. It holds the business name, category, address or service area, hours, photos, reviews and posts. A complete, accurate and active profile is one of the most important factors in appearing for local searches. Read the guide.

Hallucination

In AI, a hallucination is output that sounds plausible but is false or unsupported, such as an invented statistic, quote, citation or product detail. It happens because language models generate likely-sounding text rather than looking facts up. Checking claims against reliable sources before using them is the standard safeguard. Read the guide.

IndexNow

IndexNow is an open protocol that lets a website notify participating search engines, such as Bing and Yandex, the moment a page is added, updated or deleted, instead of waiting to be recrawled. It works by sending a simple request with the changed URLs and a key file hosted on the site. Google does not currently participate.

JSON-LD

JSON-LD, JavaScript Object Notation for Linked Data, is a format for adding structured data to a page inside a script tag, separate from the visible HTML. Google recommends it as the preferred way to add schema markup because it is easier to write and maintain than markup woven through the page. Read the guide.

Landing page

A landing page is a web page built for a single purpose, usually where visitors arrive from an ad, email or search result. It focuses on one offer and one action, with a clear headline, supporting proof and a prominent call to action, and minimal distractions such as unrelated navigation links. Read the guide.

Large language model

A large language model is an AI system trained on very large amounts of text to predict and generate language. It powers chat assistants that draft, summarise, translate and answer questions. Such models can write fluently but may state incorrect things confidently, so important outputs should be checked by a person. Read the guide.

llms.txt

llms.txt is a proposed convention for a plain-text or Markdown file at a website's root that summarises the site and points AI models to its most useful pages. It is not an official standard, and support among AI providers is limited and uncertain, so it is best treated as an optional, low-cost addition. Read the guide.

Local pack

Local pack is another name for the group of map-linked business listings that search engines show for local queries. The term is often used interchangeably with map pack. Businesses improve their chances of appearing through accurate profiles, relevant categories, reviews, consistent NAP details and locally relevant website content. Read the guide.

LTV

LTV, customer lifetime value, estimates the total revenue or, better, gross profit a business can expect from a customer over the whole relationship. It reflects order value, repeat purchases and retention. Businesses compare it with customer acquisition cost to decide how much they can sensibly spend to win a customer.

Map pack

The map pack is the block of local business results, usually three, shown with a map near the top of Google's results for searches with local intent, such as a service plus a city. Appearing there depends largely on relevance, distance and prominence, including the strength of a business's Google Business Profile. Read the guide.

NAP

NAP stands for name, address and phone number. For local search, a business's NAP should be identical everywhere it appears: its website, Google Business Profile, directories and social profiles. Consistent details help search engines confirm the business is real and located where it says, which supports local rankings. Read the guide.

One-click unsubscribe

One-click unsubscribe lets a recipient opt out of marketing email with a single action, usually through a List-Unsubscribe header that the inbox shows as a button. Major mailbox providers such as Gmail and Yahoo require it for bulk senders of marketing email, and unsubscribe requests should be honoured promptly. Read the guide.

Prompt

A prompt is the instruction or question given to an AI model. Clear prompts state the task, the audience, the format wanted and any facts the model must use. Including examples and constraints usually produces more useful output than a short, vague request, and reduces the need for heavy editing afterwards. Read the guide.

Retainer

A retainer is a fixed monthly fee paid to an agency or consultant for an agreed scope of ongoing work, such as managing ads, producing content or SEO. It gives both sides predictable planning. A good retainer agreement lists the deliverables the fee buys, the reporting and the notice needed to end it. Read the guide.

ROAS

ROAS, return on ad spend, is revenue attributed to advertising divided by the cost of that advertising. A ROAS of 3 means three dollars of revenue per dollar spent. Because it uses revenue rather than profit, and depends on attribution settings, a business should judge it against its margins. Read the guide.

Schema markup

Schema markup is structured data added to a web page, using the shared Schema.org vocabulary, to describe what the page is about: a product, a business, an article, an FAQ, an event. It helps search engines and other systems understand content and can make pages eligible for enhanced results. Read the guide.

Sender reputation

Sender reputation is the trust mailbox providers place in a sending domain and IP address, based on signals such as spam complaints, bounces, engagement, authentication and sending patterns. A good reputation helps email reach the inbox; a poor one sends it to spam or gets it blocked. It builds slowly and can be damaged quickly. Read the guide.

SOW

An SOW, statement of work, is a document describing a specific project or engagement: deliverables, timeline, responsibilities, acceptance criteria and fees. It often sits under a broader services agreement that holds the legal terms. A clear SOW prevents disputes by turning verbal promises into written commitments. Read the guide.

Spam complaint rate

Spam complaint rate is the share of delivered emails that recipients mark as spam. Mailbox providers weigh it heavily. Google's sender guidelines ask bulk senders to keep it low, generally below 0.3 percent and ideally much lower. Clear consent, relevant content and an easy unsubscribe help keep complaints down. Read the guide.

SPF

Sender Policy Framework is an email authentication standard published as a DNS text record. It lists which mail servers are allowed to send email for your domain, so receiving servers can check whether a message claiming to be from you came from an approved source. It is one of the three core authentication records alongside DKIM and DMARC. Read the guide.

Warm-up

Warm-up is the practice of gradually increasing the volume of email sent from a new domain, mailbox or IP address over several weeks. Starting small and building steadily lets mailbox providers observe good behaviour before trusting larger volumes, reducing the chance that a sudden spike of email is treated as spam. Read the guide.
