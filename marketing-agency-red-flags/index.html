What are the red flags when hiring a marketing agency?

Guaranteed results, owning your accounts, vague scope and the other warning signs worth walking away from.

SmallBizWire Editorial Team · 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: The biggest red flags are guaranteed results or rankings, an agency that wants your ad accounts or domain in its name, vague scope with no written deliverables, long lock-in contracts, no named people on your account, reports full of vanity metrics, unverifiable case studies and pressure to sign quickly. Any one deserves a direct question; several together mean walk away.

Most agencies are honest businesses doing difficult work. But marketing is easy to sell and hard to measure, and small businesses are the most exposed because they rarely have an in-house expert checking the agency's claims. These warning signs are worth knowing before the sales call, not after the first invoice.

Do they guarantee results?

Be very wary of guarantees such as "first page of Google in 30 days," "3x return on ad spend," or a fixed number of leads. Google itself states that no one can guarantee a ranking. Ad results depend on your product, price, stock, website and how fast you answer customers, none of which the agency controls. An honest agency commits to a measure, a target and a date to review, and explains what could stop it being hit.

Do they want to own your accounts?

If an agency insists on creating your ad account, analytics, domain or store under its own name, stop. You lose your data, audiences and history if you leave, and it gives the agency leverage over you. Legitimate agencies work inside accounts you own, added as a partner or user.

Is the scope vague?

"Full social media management" or "SEO services" with no numbers is not a scope. You should be able to read exactly what you get each month: how many posts, videos, campaigns, articles, reports and meetings. If the agency resists writing it down, the vagueness is the business model. Our contract checklist shows what a clear scope includes.

Is there a long lock-in?

A minimum term to give paid campaigns time to learn is reasonable. A twelve-month contract with heavy exit penalties and automatic renewal, for a small business trying an agency for the first time, is not. Ask for a shorter initial term or a clear exit if agreed targets are missed.

Do you know who will actually do the work?

A common pattern is a senior team in the pitch and juniors on the account. Ask who will run your work day to day, what their experience is, and whether you can meet them. If the answer is "a dedicated team" with no names, keep asking.

Are the case studies verifiable?

Results presented without client names, dates, context or references cannot be checked. That does not prove they are false, since some clients require confidentiality, but it means you should ask to speak with a current client in a business similar to yours. Be cautious of review profiles full of short, similar five-star reviews posted within a short period.

Are the reports full of vanity metrics?

Impressions, reach, followers and "engagement" can all rise while sales stay flat. A good report leads with the measures that connect to revenue: leads, orders, cost per acquisition, return on ad spend, and how they moved against the agreed target. If the agency cannot explain how its numbers relate to your sales, that is a warning.

Are they marking up ad spend in secret?

Some agencies pay ad platforms themselves and invoice you a higher amount without saying so. Insist that ad spend is billed by the platform to your own payment method, or that any management fee on spend is stated plainly.

Are they pressuring you to sign?

"This price is only valid today," or a discount for signing on the call, are sales tactics, not partnership signals. A good agency will give you time to read the contract and check references.

Which tactics should worry you most?

  • Buying followers, reviews or links. Fake reviews break platform rules and, in the US, can draw regulator attention; bought links can harm search rankings.
  • Black-hat SEO such as hidden text, link schemes or mass-produced pages with no value.
  • Spammy cold email from your domain with no authentication or opt-out, which can damage your sender reputation. Our guide to SPF, DKIM and DMARC explains why.
  • Asking for your main passwords instead of using platform access tools.

How long should you give an agency before judging it?

Give paid campaigns around three months and SEO longer, but judge communication from week one. Missed deadlines, unanswered questions and reports that arrive late or say little are early signals that rarely improve on their own. Raise them directly, in writing, and note the response.

What should you do if you already hired the wrong agency?

First, make sure you hold owner access to every account: ad accounts, analytics, domain, hosting and store. If you do not, ask for ownership to be transferred before you raise anything else. Then download your reports, creative and files. Give notice in writing, following the contract's terms, and remove the agency's access on the agreed date. Change any passwords that were shared. Finally, write down what went wrong, so the next brief and contract cover it. Ending a poor engagement quickly usually costs less than hoping it improves.

What are the green flags?

The opposite of each warning: questions about your margins and customers before any proposal, a written scope, accounts in your name, named people, reports tied to revenue, honest talk about what might not work, and a willingness to start small. Questions readers ask

Is it a red flag if an agency guarantees first-page Google rankings?

Yes. Google states that no one can guarantee a ranking, so a guarantee suggests either misunderstanding or risky tactics.

Should an agency own my ad account?

No. The account should be in your business's name, with the agency added as a partner or user.

Are long agency contracts always bad?

Not always, but a long first contract with heavy exit penalties and automatic renewal is a warning sign for a small business.

How can I check an agency's case studies?

Ask to speak with a current client in a business like yours, and look for named, dated results with context.

What should an agency report each month?

Measures tied to revenue, such as leads, orders, cost per acquisition and return on ad spend, against an agreed target.
